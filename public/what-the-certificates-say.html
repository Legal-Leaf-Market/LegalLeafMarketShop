Our own research

what thecertificates say

We read the paperwork. All of it. 1,550 reports, and a sum that has to come out right

Nobody writes this piece except a price comparison site, because nobody else has any reason to read this many lab reports in a row. Shops publish their own. Buyers read one. We had to read every certificate every store publishes, in order to put a number on a card, and reading them in bulk shows you things that reading one never will. Some of it is very good. Some of it is not.

I

the useful accident

Every certificate carries a checksum, and almost nobody uses it

A cannabis certificate reports THCA, delta-9 THC, and a figure called Total THC. Those three numbers are not independent. Total THC is derived from the other two, by the decarboxylation mass ratio explained in The Seam:

Total THC = (THCA × 0.877) + delta-9 THC

Which means a certificate has to agree with itself. And that turns a document you would otherwise have to trust into a document you can test.

We apply that test to every record before it is allowed near a product card. If the three numbers do not close to within a small tolerance, we do not publish a corrected version and we do not publish our best guess. We throw the record away. The product then shows no potency figure at all and drops out of the strength sorting and filters entirely.

Across the corpus, the honest certificates close to a median deviation of about 0.001 percentage points. That is not "close enough". That is the labs' own arithmetic being exactly right, which is what makes the handful of failures so conspicuous.

You cannot argue with a sum.
That is the entire reason we trust any of this.

II

the corpus

What we are actually holding

Two bodies of certificates back what you see on the site, built at different times and by different methods, because the stores publish in wildly different ways. A third is archived: the store it came from was delisted, so its records no longer sit behind any card. It is listed here because what it taught us about reading certificates still stands.

corpusrecordshow they were obtained
General 1,220 Certificates published per product, scraped from the stores' own images and PDFs.
Black Tie 146 Extracted from PDFs linked inside each product description.
Archived 184 A store that published nothing scrapeable at all, no COA link, no COA image, no mention. This corpus came from the shop's own certificate folder, matched to products by strain name. That store is no longer listed here, so these records back nothing you can buy; they are kept for reference.

That last row is worth pausing on, delisted or not. Sixty-four of those certificates are scans or photographs with no text layer, no machine-readable text to parse at all. There was no optical character recognition available on the machine doing the work, so they were rendered as images and read by eye, one at a time, and typed in.

Which sounds like exactly the kind of process you should not trust. And it would be, except that every single one still had to satisfy the checksum in chapter I before it was allowed to exist. A mis-read digit does not close the sum. Of those 64, the transcriptions closed to a median deviation of 0.0014 and a worst case of 0.0117. The arithmetic is what makes hand-read numbers publishable; the care is not sufficient on its own.

Those records were flagged on the card as read from a scan and carried a lower confidence score, because how a number was obtained is part of what the number means. With that corpus archived, nothing currently on the site carries a hand-read number: every figure you see now came out of a machine-readable certificate.

III

the big one

One sheet, forty-two products, thirty-seven different strains

This is the finding that changed how the whole site works.

A single certificate was attached to 42 products spanning 37 distinct strains, John Truffolta, Royal Gorilla, Skywalker OG, Sour Tangie, and thirty-three others. Every one of them carrying the same sample name, the same date, and the same potency: 30.84% THCA.

That is not a lab result. Thirty-seven different cultivars do not test identically. It is a store-level compliance document, one sheet the vendor hangs on many listings, which is a perfectly normal thing for a vendor to hold and a completely invalid basis for a potency claim about any individual strain.

And before we caught it, our own site was making that claim. The card said lab verified. The number fed the strength sorting and the minimum-THC filter. All of it wrong, and wrong in the direction of a specific, measured-sounding assertion about potency, which is the worst direction to be wrong in.

A number with a lab's name on it is not a measurement.
It is a measurement of something. The question is what.

The fix is mechanical rather than editorial. We group every record by the sheet it came from, and if one sheet backs more than one distinct strain, it is a store document and no product gets a potency figure from it. Several pack sizes of the same strain sharing a sheet is legitimate and stays, which is why the test keys on the strain name and not on the raw product count.

Across the live catalogue that currently demotes about 136 products. They lose the badge, the figure and their place in the sorting, and their panel says plainly that no report has been tied to that listing. The certificate link stays, because the document is real; it just is not about that flower.

IV

three kinds of document

"Has a COA" is not one fact

When a shop says a product has a certificate, that can mean three genuinely different things, and we found all three in quantity:

kindlive countwhat it actually is
product1,273 A certificate for this item alone. The real thing.
store117 One compliance sheet spread across many listings. See chapter III.
index603 A link to the shop's lab-results page. A directory, not a result.

Before we separated these, the card printed "lab-tested" whenever a certificate link was merely present. On 404 products that produced a card face claiming lab verification directly above a panel that truthfully said no report had been published for that item. The page contradicted itself, and the flattering half was in the bigger type.

Now the card says which of the three it is: view COA, store COA, or lab results. Three words of honesty, and it cost nothing.

The one exception we allow

A shop that publishes a certificate for every product, and where we have successfully parsed hundreds of them, has demonstrably tested its range. If we then fail to match one particular strain to its sheet, that is a gap in our matching rather than evidence the batch was never tested, so the badge stays, on the strength of the store's record, while the numbers still do not. Three shops currently meet that bar. Several conspicuously do not, including one with 107 certificate links from which we have never once managed to parse a result.

V

the smaller landmines

Things a lab report will do to you

A list, because each of these was a real bug that produced a real wrong number, and each was caught by the checksum rather than by anybody being clever.

  • The same analyte printed twice. One lab repeats its analyte table on a later quality-control page with different figures. Reading the document front-to-back returned a THCA of 3.72% for flower. Reading only the pages before the quality-control heading moved the median from 1.54% to 26.90%.
  • Four numbers in a row, and the first one is not the answer. Cannabinoid rows often run limit-of-detection, limit-of-quantitation, percent, milligrams per gram. Taking the first number gave a THCA of 0.0024% on sixteen reports.
  • Terpene tables have one fewer column than cannabinoid tables. Which published a limonene figure of 5.9% on a sample whose total terpenes were 1.97%, a number that would be remarkable if it were real. The robust fix is to take the last two numbers on the row and require that the milligrams-per-gram figure is ten times the percentage. If it is not, the row has been misread.
  • Two-column layouts flattened into one line. One lab's PDF interleaves two columns, so an analyte name ends up glued to the summary figure from the other side of the page. This one passed the ten-times check, because the pair it produced was a genuine pair, from the wrong row.
  • An optional letter in a pattern. Searching for "THCa" with an optional final letter also matches a bare "THC", so on a line reading Delta9 THC 0.26% THCa 27.76% it returned 0.26 as the THCA. Fifty-nine records failed the checksum on that one, which is exactly what the checksum is for.

Every one of those is a way to publish a confident, specific, entirely wrong potency. None of them was caught by looking at the output and thinking it seemed plausible, because all of them did seem plausible. They were caught by a sum.

VI

the one that is not a bug

Two strains, one analysis

And then there is the finding that no amount of better parsing fixes.

Two entirely different strains from one shop, different names, different listings, carry certificates with the same sample identifier and byte-for-byte identical values. Same THCA, same delta-9, same total, same date. One analysis, filed twice under two names.

One of those two products is described by a lab result that was not taken from it. We have no way to determine which, so neither of them gets lab data. Both were dropped.

Notably, our own shared-sheet detection from chapter III would have missed this, because that test fingerprints partly on the sample name, and here the names differ. It took a separate check, fingerprinting on the values alone, to see it. Five matching numbers across two unrelated cultivars does not happen by chance.

We are not naming the strains or the lab here. The point is not to pillory one laboratory over what may well be a clerical error; the point is that if you read enough certificates you will find this, and that a shop's job is to drop the record rather than pick the more flattering half.

VII

what we think this means

The paperwork is better than its reputation, and worse than its marketing

The honest summary of 1,550 certificates is not a scandal, and we are not going to manufacture one. The overwhelming majority are internally consistent to three decimal places. Labs like PharmLabs, Excelbis and the others whose reports make up this corpus are, on the evidence of their own arithmetic, doing careful work.

The problem is almost never the laboratory. It is the distance between the certificate and the product page, a sheet that belongs to a different strain, a link that goes to a directory, a badge printed because a field was non-empty. Every failure we found lives in that gap, and every one of them flattered the seller.

The lab did its job.
The gap between the lab and the label is where the truth goes missing.

Which is why the numbers on this site are gated the way they are, and why a product with no certificate of its own shows nothing rather than something. An empty field is not a failure of a comparison engine. It is the engine refusing to make something up.

Notes on method

  1. Figures in this piece are counts from our own live catalogue and certificate corpora at the time of writing, August 2026. Inventory moves daily, so the product counts drift; the record counts do not.
  2. The identity test is Total THC = (THCA × 0.877) + delta-9 THC, accepted within the greater of 1.5 percentage points or 8% of the reported total. Records failing it are discarded, not corrected.
  3. The 0.877 factor is the ratio of molar masses, Δ9-THC ≈ 314.5 ÷ THCA ≈ 358.5.
  4. Shared-sheet detection groups records by sample name, date, THCA, total and laboratory, then demotes any sheet backing more than one distinct strain name. Reused analyses are caught by a second pass fingerprinting on values alone.
  5. Certificate scope is classified as product, store or index by whether the linked asset is a document, how many distinct strains share it, and whether it resolves to a file or a page.

Legal-Leaf Market is a price comparison service. We hold no stock and sell nothing. Nothing here is medical advice or a claim about any product's effects. Where we describe a certificate as inconsistent we mean its own reported figures do not reconcile; we make no allegation of intent by any laboratory or retailer. Adults 21+ only.
